\begin{abstract}
How well known a real number is can be specified by a rational interval that contains the real number. Flipping this leads to defining a real number by a rational betweenness relation. Specifically, two rational numbers are $x$-related if $x$ ought to be, inclusively, between them. These betweenness relations tap into the long history of using rational intervals to define real numbers. The distinction here is the simple properties that characterize such relations. The key property is the Separation property which states that given two rationals in relation, then any number in between is either $x$-related to exactly one of the two endpoints or is $x$-related to itself. After establishing the customary ordered field structures, a canonical field isomorphism is established with Dedekind cuts. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rational intervals as definitions of real numbers has a long history, starting with Bachmann's nested intervals in the late 1800s as mentioned in the real numbers overview of Ittay Weiss \cite{ittay-2015}. More recently, the constructivists, as explored by Mark Bridger \cite{bridger}, consider families of overlapping, arbitrarily small closed intervals. There are also more topologically related approaches using open intervals such as in negri and in asd and minimal cauchy filters...

For some reason, there has been little use in defining real numbers as rational intervals in this way in the classroom. The hope of this paper is that the definition presented here is elementary and enticing enough to get more general adoption as a real number definition. It has a natural presentation of giving information about a real number as being between two rational numbers. This replaces the usual ``$\pi$
is approximately $3.14$'' with ``$\pi$ is known to be between $3.14$ and $3.15$''.  The latter is a crisp, explicit statement with real content. The former is common but only has meaning if one understands it to mean that $\pi$ being approximately $3.14$ has an implication of being between $3.135$ to $3.145$. By focusing on intervals as the very foundation of real numbers, it encourages better habits of approximate understanding. 

The definition in this paper has the virtue of having no equivalence classes to discuss; there is exactly one rational betweenness relation which represents a given real number $x$. It also facilitates direct arithmetic computation using interval arithmetic. The properties defining the relations are easy to understand. Existence demands that there is at least one interval containing the real number. Separation is the ability to divide that interval into a smaller interval that contains the real number. Those two combine to already give the necessary ingredients to refine the knowledge of the number to as arbitrarily small interval width as desired and computationally feasible. Consistency is the simple observation that if an interval contains the real number, then any other interval containing that interval also contains the real number. The final two properties, Singular and Inward, ensure that rational numbers are represented correctly by the relations. 

The approach of using closed intervals to represent real numbers is done so that rational real numbers can be represented by the singleton consisting of just the rational number itself. This makes arithmetic exactly if dealing with rational numbers. The difficulty is that, in general, a real number that is rational cannot always be established to be that rational number. This is the standard decidability problem. This paper ignores this and assumes decidability in all instances is possible. For a related approach that gives a stronger ability to discuss decidability, see \cite{taylor24oraclerbr}.

Throughout this paper and generally without further explicit comment, the letters $a, b, c, d$ will always be rational numbers and often take the role of endpoints of intervals. The letters $m, n, p, q, r, s$ will also always be rational numbers and often take the role of numbers in an interval. The symbols $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ will be non-negative rational numbers. The letters $x, y, z, \alpha, \beta$ will notionally represent real numbers.

 A \textbf{rational interval} $a \rint b$ is the set of all rationals $q$ such that $q$ is between $a$ and $b$, inclusively. A \textbf{rational singleton} $a:a$ is a set consisting of just $a$ and it is accepted as a rational interval. The empty set is not a rational interval. 

Similar notation will be used to indicate betweenness such as $a \rint b\rint c$ implying $b$ is, inclusively, between $a$ and $c$. It is easy to extend this notation such as $a \rint b \rint c \rint d$ implying either $a \leq b \leq c \leq d$ or $d \leq c \leq b \leq a$. Replacing $\rint$ with $\betneq$ implies strict inequality. A bracketed expression in a chain such as $\{b, c\}$ is used to indicate common betweenness relations around $b$ and $c$ without deciding the relative ordering of $b$ and $c$. Thus, $a \betneq \{b, c \} \rint d$ would be interpreted as $a$ is not equal to $b$ or $c$ and that both $b$ and $c$ are between $a$ and $d$. As an illustration, a strictly monotonic function $f$ can be described as one in which $a \betneq c \betneq b$ implies $f(a) \betneq f(c) \betneq f(b)$ for all $a,b,c$ in the domain of $f$, assuming at least three points in the domain. Replacing $\betneq$ with $\rint$ gives the monotonic functions. 

If $b$ is not between $a$ and $c$, this could be indicated either by \sout{$a \rint b\rint c$} or by $b\rint \{a,c\}$.

\section{Definition of a Rational Betweenness Relation}

A rational betweenness relation is a relation on pairs of rational numbers that is supposed to express that there is a real number $x$ between the two rational numbers. The notation $a \xrel b$ denotes that $x$ ought to satisfy $a \leq x \leq b$ or $b \leq x \leq a$;  both the ordered pairs $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$ are then in the betweenness relation. The interval $a \rint b$ is then an \textbf{$x$-interval} and $a$ is \textbf{$x$-related} to $b$. If $(a,b)$ is not in the relation, then that can be denoted by \sout{$a \xrel b$}. 

Since this is defining the real number $x$, the above is only a notional idea behind the definition. The definition is that a \textbf{rational betweenness relation} is a symmetric relation on rational numbers 
which satisfies the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Existence. There exist $a$ and $b$ such that $a\xrel b$.
    \item Separation. If $a \xrel b$ and $a \rint  c \rint  b$, then exactly one of the following holds: 1) $a \xrel c$ and \sout{$c \xrel b$}, 2) $c \xrel b$ and \sout{$a \xrel c$}, or 3) $c \xrel c$. 
    \item Consistency. If $c \rint  a \rint b \rint  d$ and $a \xrel b$, then $c \xrel d$. 
    \item Singular. If $c \xrel c$ and $d \xrel d$, then $c=d$. 
    \item Inward. If \sout{$a \xrel a$} and $a \xrel b$, then there exists $c \neq a$ between $a$ and $b$ such that $c \xrel b$. 
\end{enumerate}

If $q \xrel q$, then $x$ represents the rational number $q$ and $q$ is the \textbf{root of the relation}.
If such a $q$ exists, then $x$ is said to be rational. Also note that the Singular property implies that $q$ would be unique. If no such $q$ exists, then $x$ is said to be irrational. 

In a betweenness chain, the presence of $\xrel$ asserts the $x$-relation for the immediate rationals on either side, but Consistency allows for the conclusion of $x$-relatedness for any pair enclosing them. For example, Inward could have its concluding clause stated as there exists $c$ such that $a \betneq c \xrel b$. That betweenness chain implies $a \xrel b$, $c \xrel b$, $a \rint c \rint b$, and $a \neq c$.

Inward can be applied twice to conclude that if $a \xrel b$ and neither endpoints are roots, then there exists $c\ xrel d$ such that $a \betneq c \rint d \betneq b$. Any interval $a \rint b$ is contained in an $x$-interval; Consistency is the main reason, but Separation is important for the case of $a \rint b$ overlapping an $x$-interval as Separation can then break the $x$-interval on the overlapping endpoint into an $x$-interval that could then be extended with Consistency to contain $a \rint b$. 

The Separation property is at the heart of this procedure being computationally useful. It is modeled on how the Intermediate Value Theorem is often operationalized. For example, if a continuous function $f \col\  \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is strictly monotonic and $f(a) \rint u \rint f(b)$ for some rational $u$, then define $x$ as $c \xrel d$ exactly when  $f(c) \rint u \rint f(d)$. The relation $x$ is a rational betweenness relation as demonstrated by checking that the required properties hold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Existence. Established with the original interval $a \rint b$ that satisfies $f(a) \rint u \rint f(b)$.
    \item Separation. Given $c \xrel d$ and $c \rint m \rint d$, then either $f(m) = u$ implying $m \xrel m$, $f(c) \rint f(m) \betneq u \rint f(d)$ implying $m \xrel d$ and \sout{$c \xrel m$}, or $f(c) \rint u \betneq f(m) \rint f(d)$ implying $c \xrel m$ and \sout{$m \xrel d$}.
    \item Consistency. From monotonicity,  if $m \rint c \rint d \rint n$ with $c \xrel d$, then $f(m) \rint f(c) \rint u \rint f(d) \rint f(n)$ which implies $m \xrel n$.
    \item Singular. Since  $c \xrel c$ holds exactly when $f(c) \rint u \rint f(c)$ which is exactly when $f(c) = u$, the strict monotonicity yields the Singular property.
    \item Inward. Continuity implies, by standard $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ arguments, that if $f(c) \betneq u \rint f(d)$, then there exists $m$ such that $c\betneq m \betneq d$ and $f(c) \rint f(m) \betneq u \rint f(d)$. This translates directly to the Inward property. 
\end{enumerate}

To describe the positive $n$-th root of the positive rational $q$, the relation is $a \xrel b$  exactly when $\max(0, a)^n \rint q \rint \max(0, b)^n$. For a Cauchy sequence whose limit is not a rational, the relation can be described prosaically as the intervals that contain some tail of the sequence. To handle rational limits as well as arithmetic, the concept of a fonsi will be introduced and explored. 

All of the properties are independent: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Existence. The empty relation satisfies the other four properties vacuously. In practical computations, Existence supplies the first interval upon which Separation then refines. 
    \item Separation. Define $a \sim b$ if there exists $c$ such that $a\betneq c \betneq b$ and $0 \betneq c \betneq 1$. Existence and Consistency apply easily. Singular holds vacuously as there are no roots. Separation fails as $0 \xrel 1$ splits into $0\xrel c$ and $c \xrel 1$ for any $0 \betneq c \betneq 1$ despite \sout{$c\xrel c$}. Inward applies as the defining $c$ is the inward point needed; it lies strictly between $0$ and $1$ and thus there is another point between $c$ and $b$ that strictly lies within $(0,1)$.
    \item Consistency. Let $x$ be a rational betweenness relation and $a \xrel b$. Then define $c \sim d$ to mean $a \rint c \rint d \rint b$ and $c \xrel d$. Then Existence, Separation, and Inward all apply to $\sim$ as those are internal to $c \rint d$. Singular applies as $\sim$ is a restriction and so cannot add anything. Consistency is the only one that fails. It should be noted that for purposes of describing a real number, the relation $\sim$ would be fine. Consistency is used to make a unique, maximal representative. It alleviates having to track whether an interval has been arbitrarily included in the definition or not. 
    \item Singular. Let $a \xrel b$ hold true if there exists $a \rint c \rint b$ such that $0 \rint c \rint 1$. All of the rationals in $0\rint 1$ would then be roots. Existence and Consistency are clear. For Inward, if $a$ is not a root, then it is outside the unit interval. One could then take the midpoint between $a$ and the closest endpoint of the unit interval as the desired $c$. Separation works because of the clause $c \xrel c$. Any separation point chosen in $0 \rint 1$ will be a root and hence the failure to create an exclusive or separate intervals is not a problem. For a $c$ outside of the unit interval, it would be on one side of that interval and thus exactly one of the divided parts meets the unit interval. 
    \item Inward. Let $a \xrel b$ with $a \leq b$ be such that $a \leq q < b$. Then \sout{$q \xrel q$}. Consistency is clear as is existence such as $q\xrel q+1$. There are no roots so Singular applies vacuously. For Separation holding, if $a \rint c \rint q \betneq b$, then $c\xrel b$ and \sout{$a \xrel c$}. If $q \betneq c \rint b$, then $a \xrel c$ and \sout{$c \xrel b$}. Inward fails as if $q < b$, then $q \xrel b$, \sout{$q \xrel q$}, and there is no $c > q$ such that $c \xrel b$.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Families of Overlapping, Notionally Shrinking Intervals}

A \textbf{Family of Overlapping, Notionally Shrinking Intervals} (fonsi) is a non-empty set of rational intervals that pairwise intersect and that there are arbitrarily small intervals in the family. As will be established, each rational betweenness relation is a maximal fonsi and all maximal fonsis are rational betwenness relations. The smallest fonsi is one consisting of just a singleton. Equivalence classes of fonsis are used by constructivists as the definition of a real number and they naturally represent ideal measurements and Cauchy sequences can be easily converted to a fonsi. Every fonsi gives rise to a unique maximal fonsi. Fonsis are the natural foundations in implementing the arithmetic of rational betweenness relations. 


\begin{lemma}[Touching]\label{br--cor--endpointed}
    If $a \xrel p$, $p \xrel b$, and $a \rint p \rint b$, then $p$ is a root.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    By Consistency, $a \xrel b$ as it contains an $x$-interval. Apply Separation to $a \rint b$ with separation point $p$. Since both $a \rint p$ and $p \rint b$ are $x$-intervals, the only compatible option is $p \xrel p$.
\end{proof}


\begin{proposition}[Intersection]\label{br--intersect}
    If $a \xrel b$ and $c \xrel d$, then $a \rint b$ and $c\rint d$ intersect in an $x$-interval. If it is also the case that  $\{a, c\} \rint \{b, d\}$, then  $c \xrel b$ and $a \xrel d$.
\end{proposition}

This uses only Separation, Consistency, and Singular. 

\begin{proof}
    Without loss of generality, it can be assumed that $a \rint \{b, c, d\}$ and $a \rint c \rint d$. 

    If $a \rint c \rint d \rint b$, then $c\rint d$ is the intersection and it is given as an $x$-interval. Both $c \xrel b$ and $a \xrel d$ follow from Consistency.

    The other possibility is that $a \rint \{b, c\} \rint d$. In this case, $a \xrel d$ by Consistency. There are three cases to consider:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $a \rint b \betneq c\rint d$. Pick any two rationals $m$ and $n$ such that $b \betneq m \betneq n \betneq c$.  By Consistency, $a \xrel m$, $d \xrel m$, $a \xrel n$, and $d \xrel n$. Then Touching yields $m \xrel m$ and $n \xrel n$. This contradicts Singular. 
        \item $a\rint (b=c)\rint d$. This is exactly Touching. 
        \item $a\rint c\betneq b\rint d$. By applying Separation, with $c$ as the Separation point in the interval $a \rint b$, since $c \xrel d$, it must be the case that either $c \xrel c$ or $c \xrel b$.
    \end{enumerate}
    Note that in the successful cases, $c \xrel b$. 
\end{proof}

This has some immediate implications

\begin{corollary}\label{lem:inter}
    Let $\xrel$ be a rational betweenness relation. Then
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*., ref=\thelemma(\alph*) ]
        \item\label{br--cor--rooted} If $q \xrel q$ and $a \xrel b$, then $a \rint q \rint b$.
        \item\label{br--cor-disj} If $a \xrel b$ and $c \rint d$ is disjoint from $a \rint b$, then \sout{$c \xrel d$}. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
        \item They must intersect.
        \item This is a restatement that $x$-intervals must intersect.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}


The Inward property is, given the other properties, equivalent to the Closed property.

\begin{proposition}[Closed]\label{br--closed} 
    If $a$ is not a root, then $a$ is not contained in some $x$-interval. 
\end{proposition}

This also implies that if $a$ is contained in every $x$-interval then it is a root. 

\begin{proof}
    By Existence, there exists interval $u \rint v$. If $a \betneq \{u, v\}$, then the conclusion is satisfied. Otherwise, $u \rint a \rint v$ and applying Separation using $a$ leads to either $a \xrel u$ or $a \xrel v$ since $a$ is not a root. Apply Inward to the $x$ interval to get an interval that does not contain $a$. 
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    If the first four properties are satisfied, then Closed and Inward are equivalent.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Inward was already used to prove Closed; this was done without Singular. 

    For the other direction, let $a \xrel b$ and $a$ is not a root. Closed implies there exists $c \xrel d$ such that $a$ is not in $c \rint d$. Intersect it with $a \xrel b$ and, without loss of generality, assume $a \betneq c \rint \{b,d\}$. Then $c \xrel b$ by Intersection. This is what was required to be shown.
\end{proof}


\begin{proposition}[Bisection]\label{br--prop--bisection}
    Given rational $\varepsilon >0$ and rational betweenness relation $\xrel$, there exists $a \rint b$ such that $|a-b| < \varepsilon$ and $a \xrel b$. 
\end{proposition}

This is the bisection algorithm as used, for example, in some proofs of the Intermediate Value Theorem. It applies to any set of intervals that satisfy Existence and Separation. 

\begin{proof}
    Let $a_1\rint b_1$ be an $x$-interval; this exists by the Existence property.
    
    Assume $a_i\rint b_i$ is defined. Let $m_i = \frac{a_i + b_i}{2}$. By Separation, using $m_i$ as the Separation point in $a_i \rint b_i$, either 1) $a_i \xrel m_i$, 2) $b_i \xrel m_i$, or 3) $m_i \xrel m_i$. If it is 3), then $m_i\rint m_i$ satisfies the requirements as its length is 0. Otherwise, for 1), let $a_{i+1}= a_i$, $b_{i+1} = m_i$ and for 2) let $a_{i+1} = m_i$, $b_{i+1} = b_i$. Then $|a_{i+1} - b_{i+1}| = 2^{-1} |a_i-b_i|= 2^{-i} |a_1-b_1|$. 

    Since there exists an $N$ such that $2^{-N} |a_1-b_1| < \varepsilon$, this process will finish in at most $N$ steps. 
\end{proof}

Intersection and Bisection establishes that each rational betweenness relation is a fonsi. A \textbf{maximal fonsi} is one in which there is no other fonsi that properly contains it. 

\begin{lemma}\label{br-lem-witness}
    If \sout{$a \xrel b$}, then there exists an $x$-interval disjoint from $a \rint b$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Since \sout{$a \xrel b$}, all subintervals, including the singletons $a\rint a$ and $b\rint b$, are not $x$-intervals by Consistency. Using Closed, there exists an $x$-interval that excludes  $a$ and there also exists an $x$-interval $b$ are not in those intervals, respectively. By Intersection, they intersect in an $x$-interval which therefore excludes $a$ and $b$. By Consistency, it cannot be contained in $a \rint b$. Thus, the intersection $x$-interval is disjoint from $a \rint b$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{br--something-outside} 
    Given a relation $\xrel$, an interval is an $x$-interval exactly when it intersects every $x$-interval. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Intersection provides one direction; the contrapositive of the lemma establishes the other direction. 
\end{proof}


\begin{lemma}\label{br-lem-same-side}
    If a fonsi $\mathcal{F}$ contains an interval $a \rint b$ and $a \rint b \betneq c \rint d$, then for every interval $m  \rint n$ of the fonsi that satisfies $|m-n| < |b-c|$, it is the case that $\{a, b, m, n\} \betneq c \rint d$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
 By the Intersection property, $a \rint b$ and $m \rint n$ must intersect. By the size restriction, this implies they are on the same side of $c \rint d$. 
\end{proof}


\begin{theorem}
    For a fonsi $\mathcal{F}$, define $x_{\mathcal{F}}$ to be the set of intervals that intersect every member of $\mathcal{F}$. Then $x_{\mathcal{F}}$ is a rational betweenness relation and is the only one that contains $\mathcal{F}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Since every element of a fonsi intersects every other element, $\mathcal{F}$ is contained in $x_{\mathcal{F}}$. It is immediate that this set satisfies the Closed property. Indeed, if $c$ is contained in every member of $x_{\mathcal{F}}$, then it is contained in every member of $\mathcal{F}$ which means $c \rint c$ intersects every member of the fonsi implying $c\rint c$ is an  $x_{\mathcal{F}}$-interval.
    
    
    The properties of a rational betweenness relation hold as follows: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Existence. Fonsis are non-empty by definition so that any element of $\mathcal{F}$ may be used. 
        
        \item Consistency. If an interval intersects every element of the fonsi then any interval that contains it will also intersect all of them. 
        
        \item Singular. Assume $c$ is a root which means it is contained in every element of the fonsi. Let $d$ be any other rational number. Take an element of the fonsi whose size is less than $|c-d|$. It must contain $c$ and therefore it cannot contain $d$. Thus, $d$ is not a root. 
        
        \item Separation. Let $a \rint b $ be an  $x_{\mathcal{F}}$-interval and $a \rint c \rint b$. If $c$ is contained in every member of $\mathcal{F}$, then it is a root and Separation is satisfied. If not, then there is an interval of $\mathcal{F}$ that does not contain $c$ and thus there is a gap between them. Any member of $\mathcal{F}$ whose size is smaller than the gap is therefore on the same side of $c$; without loss of generality, let's take that side to be the side of $a$. Since it must also meet $a\rint b$, it must intersect $a \rint c$ and does not intersect $b \rint c$. For any other member of the fonsi, it must intersect $a \rint b$ and the small members implying that it either is wholly intersecting $a \rint c$ or it contains $c$ as it reaches the small members which implies it also intersects $a \rint c$. In any event, $a \rint c$ is an $x_\mathcal{F}$ interval while $b \rint c$ is not since it misses the small elements of the fonsi. 
        
        \item Inward. With the other properties established, Inward is equivalent to Closed which was already established. 
    \end{enumerate}

    As for uniqueness, let $y$ be any rational betweenness relation that contains $\mathcal{F}$. Since every interval in $y$ meets every other interval in $y$ such as those in $\mathcal{F}$. Thus, every interval in $y$ is in $x_{\mathcal{F}}$. If there exists an interval $I$ in $x_{\mathcal{F}}$ not contained in $y$, then Lemma~\ref{br-lem-witness} produces an interval $J$ in $y$ disjoint from $I$. But as every interval in $y$ is in $x_{\mathcal{F}}$, it must intersect $I$ per Intersection.
\end{proof}

This has some useful corollaries: 
\begin{corollary}
   \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*., ref=\thelemma(\alph*) ]
    \item Rational Betweenness Relations are exactly the maximal fonsis. 
    \item If every element of fonsi $\mathcal{F}$ intersects every element of fonsi $\mathcal{G}$, then $x_{\mathcal{F}} = x_{\mathcal{G}}$.
    \item If the fonsi $\mathcal{G}$ is contained in $\mathcal{F}$ and an interval intersects every element of $\mathcal{G}$, then it meets every element in $\mathcal{F}$ and is in the generated relation. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
   \begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*.]
    \item If $\mathcal{M}$ is a maximal fonsi, then $x_{\mathcal{M}}$ contains $\mathcal{M}$. Since $x_{\mathcal{M}}$ is a fonsi, maximality of $\mathcal{M}$ implies $\mathcal{M} = x_{\mathcal{M}}$.
    \item The union of $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{G}$ satisfies being a fonsi. Since $x_{\mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{G}}$ contains both $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{G}$, their generated relations are identical. 
    \item If $\mathcal{G} \subset \mathcal{F}$, then their union is $\mathcal{F}$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

Singular fonsis, namely a fonsi consisting of a single element $q \rint q$ may be used to conveniently handle representing the rationals. The only way to have a finite fonsi is to have a singleton as member. 

Cauchy sequences are easily viewed as fonsis. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the set of (finite rational) intervals that contain a tail of the Cauchy sequence. As Cauchy sequences are bounded, this is nonempty. They clearly intersect. The Cauchy Criterion implies that there are arbitrarily small intervals that contain tails of the sequence. Being a fonsi, this generates a rational betweenness relation and that is the limit. 

For the earlier example involving monotonic functions, fonsis allow for the removal of the continuity requirement. Indeed, let $f: \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}$ be strictly monotonic on $a \rint b$ and that $f(a) \rint u f(b)$ for some rational $u$. Then define the fonsi to be the intervals $c \rint d$ such that $a  \rint c \rint d \rint b$ and $f(c) \rint u \rint f(b)$. The usual Intermediate Value Theorem bisection algorithm leads to this set of intervals having arbitrarily small intervals. Monotonicity gives the intersection by considering $c \rint d \betneq m \rint n$ then $f(c) \rint u \rint f(d) \rint f(m) \rint f(n)$ implies $m \rint n$ is not in the fonsi. Thus, all intervals in the fonsi must intersect.  The generated relation then marks the spot that $u$ fits in the range. At jumps, one relation will mark the spot for multiple values in the range.

Two rational betweenness relations $x$ and $y$ are different if there exists at least one interval $a \rint b$ such that either $a \xrel b$ with \sout{$a \xrel[y] b$} or $a \xrel[y] b$ with \sout{$a \xrel b$}. By the fact that an interval is in a relation if and only if it intersects all of the intervals in the relation, for two different relations each relation must contain an interval disjoint from an interval in the other relation. Since disjoint intervals can be ordered by their nearest endpoints, this forms a natural ordering on the rational betweenness relations with equality occurring only when they are truly identical. 

A relation $x$ is less than a relation $y$ exactly when there exist intervals $a \xrel b$ and $c \yrel d$ such that $a \rint b \betneq c \rint d$ and $b < c$. Equality occurs when there are no disjoint intervals which is the same as saying that they are exactly the same relations. Since small enough intervals in a relation must all be on the same side of an interval not in the relation, the ordering of the relations is independent of the representative intervals chosen to exemplify it. It is also the case that if $a \xrel b$, then $\xrel[a] \leq \xrel \leq \xrel[b]$. Inward establishes that with equality happening only if one of the endpoints is a root. 

\begin{lemma}[Transitivity]
For rational betweenness relations $x$ and $y$, if $x < y$ and $y < x$, then $x < z$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    By assumption there exist intervals $a \xrel b$, $c \yrel d$, $m \yrel n$, and $u \xrel[z] v$ such that $a \leq b < c \leq d$, $m \leq n < u \leq v$. Since $c\rint d$ and $m \rint n$ are intervals in the same relation, they must intersect in a point $p$. By the known inequalities, $b < p < u$ and thus $a \rint b < u \rint v$ establishing the relation inequality. 
\end{proof}

In practical work, a real number is often computed using means equivalent to generating fonsis. Two fonsis can be ordered if there are intervals found that are disjoint. Equality, however, cannot generally be established through finite computational means. A proof is often required to establish that the elements of both fonsis always overlap. 

The concept of betweenness also follows, either from the ordering or from the same disjoint interval notions. Either way, $x \rint y \rint z$ is sensible as an assertions that either  $ x \leq y \leq z$  or $x \geq y \geq z$ for relations $x$, $y$, and $z$. For a rational $q$, $\qrel{q}$ will be used to denote the unique rational betweenness relation whose root is $q$. By Inward, if $a \xrel b$, then $\qrel{a} \rint x \rint \qrel{b}$. For a relation $x$, using $\qrel{x}$ may be used for emphasis but is redundant.

With betweenness established, showing that the relations are complete in terms of bounded sets can now be explored. Instead of favoring an ordering, such as least upper bound, a more neutral terminology will be used. A set $S$ with a betweenness notion has a block $x$ if $S \rint x$. This is expressing all elements of the set $S$ are on the same side of $x$. Explicitly, this is quantified as for all pairs $y, z \in S$, $\{y, z\} \rint x$. It can be that $S$ is blocked on both sides or just one side. Two blocks $x$, $y$ are on the same side of $S$ if $S \rint \{x, y\}$ and are on opposite sides if $x \rint S \rint y$. A tight block $x$ of a set $S$ is a block such that for any $y$ that is a block on the same side of $S$ as $x$, then $S \rint x \rint y$. 


\begin{proposition}[Tight Block]
    Given a nonempty set $S$ of rational betweenness relations that has a block $y$, then there exists a tight block $x$ on the same side of $S$ as $y$. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $A$ be the set of blocks on the same side as $y$. Then any element of $S$ is a block of $A$ and they are all on the same side. Let $B$ be the set of blocks of $A$ on the same side as $S$. $A$ is then also a set of blocks of $B$. The tight block of $S$ on the same side as $y$ is defined as the relation $x$ such that $a \xrel b$ if $\qrel{a} \in A$ and $\qrel{b} \in B$. 

A rational $q$ is a root of $x$ if and only if $\qrel{q} \in A \cap B$. A rational $q$ forms a tight block of $S$ on the same side of $y$ as $S$  if and only if $q$ is a root of $x$. If $q$ is not a tight block of $S$, but is a block, then there exists $z$ such that $S \rint z \betneq \qrel{q}$. Then $\qrel{q}$ is not in $B$ as it is not a block of $A$ and thus it is not a root. Similarly, if $q$ is not a block of $S$ on the same side as $y$, then there exists $s \in S$ such that $\qrel{q} \rint s \rint y$ and $\qrel{q}$ is not in $A$ as it is not a block of $B$. Thus, $q$ is not a root of $x$ in this instance either. 

The union of $A$ and $B$ is the set of all relations. This follows from the fact that if $z$ is not a block of $S$ on the same side of $y$, then there exists $s \in S$ such that $z \betneq s \rint y$. But this implies all elements of $t \in A$ satisfy $z \betneq s \rint \{t, y\}$ and thus $z$ is a block on $A$ on the same side as $S$ and $z \in B$. 

Given a block $z$ of a set $T$, then for any $t \in T$ such that $t \neq z$, there exists $a \xrel[t] b$ and $c \xrel[z] d$ such that $a \rint b \betneq c \rint d$. Then $\qrel{d}$ is a block of $T$ on the same side as $z$. Indeed, let $t'$ be any element of $T$ and let $a' \xrel[t'] b'$ and $c' \xrel d'$ be such that $a' \rint b' \rint c' \rint d'$ which is allowed by $z$ being a block on $T$. As $z$ is a relation, $c \rint d$ and $c' \rint d'$. This implies $\{c, c'\} \rint d$ as otherwise $c \rint d \betneq c'\rint d'$ and they would not intersect. Therefore, $a' \rint b' \rint c' \rint d$. As $t'$ was arbitrary, it is clear that $\qrel{d}$ is a block on the same side of $T$ as $z$.  

That this is a rational betweenness relation is as follows: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Existence. Both $A$ and $B$ are nonempty. Taking a relation from each, look at their blocking interval endpoints away from the block side. As above, their relational versions are therefore blocks as well. Thus, they can serve as endpoints for a tight block relation interval. 
    \item Separation. Let $a \xrel b$ be given with $\qrel{a} \in A$ and $\qrel{b} \in B$. Take $a \rint c \rint b$. If $c$ is a root, then Separation is satisfied. If not, then from above, either $\qrel{c}$ is in $A$ but not in $B$ or it is the reverse. For the first, by definition, $c \xrel b$ and \sout{$a \xrel c$}. For the other, $a \xrel c$ and \sout{$c \xrel b$}. Thus, Separation holds. 
    \item Consistency. Let $a \xrel b$ with $\qrel{a} \in A$ and $\qrel{b} \in B$. This implies that if $u, v \in B$ then $\qrel{a} \rint \{u, \qrel{b}, v\}$; also $u', v' \in A$ implies $\qrel{b} \rint \{u', v', \qrel{a}\}$. Take $c \rint a \rint b \rint d$. Then $ \qrel{c}\rint \qrel{a} \rint \{u, \qrel{b}, v\}$  implying $\qrel{c}$ is a block of $B$;  $\qrel{d} \rint \qrel{b} \rint \{u', v', \qrel{a}\}$ implying $\qrel{d}$ is a block of $A$. Thus, $c \xrel d$.
    \item Singular. If $c \xrel c$ and $d \xrel d$, then $\qrel{c}$ and $\qrel{d}$ are both elements of $A$ and $B$. Let $\qrel{a} \in A$ and $\qrel{b} \in B$. Then as $c$ and $d$ are blocks, it is that case that $\{a, \qrel{c}\} \rint \qrel{d}$ and $\{a, \qrel{d}\} \rint \qrel{c}$. This can only happen if $c = d$. 
    \item Inward.  Let $a \xrel b$ with $\qrel{a} \in A$ and $\qrel{b} \in B$. Assume $a$ is not a root. Then by above, $\qrel{a}$ is not a tight block on $B$ implying there is a block $u$ of $B$ such that $\qrel{a} \betneq u \rint B$. Let $c \xrel[u] d$ be such that $a \betneq c \rint d$. This is what not equal implies. Then since $\qrel{c} \in A$ also from above, $c \xrel b$. A similar flow works for $B$ as well. 
    
\end{enumerate}

The only thing left to show is that $x$ is a tight block of $S$. Suppose it is not. Let $z$ be a block of of $S$ such that $S \rint z \betneq x$. Let $a \xrel b$ and $c \xrel[z] d$ such that $c \rint d \betneq a \rint b$. From above, $\qrel{d}$ is a block of $S$ so that $S \rint d \{a, b\}$. But this implies that $\qrel{a}$ and $\qrel{b}$ are both not in $B$. This contradicts the definition of $x$. 
    
\end{proof}

\section{Dedekind cuts}

A Dedekind cut models real numbers as a dividing point between rationals. There are multiple definitions. The one used here is a symmetric definition. 

A Dedekind cut is defined as a triplet of sets of rationals, $(A, B, C)$, such that: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Nonempty. Both $A$ and $C$ are non-empty.
    \item Ordered. If $a \in A$ and $c \in C$ then $a < c$. If $b \in B$, then also $a < b < c$. 
    \item Comprehensive. $A \cup B \cup C = \mathbb{Q}$.
    \item Singular. There is at most one element in $B$.
    \item Open. There is no greatest element in $A$ and no least element in $C$.
\end{enumerate}
If the cut ought to represent $x$, then the notation that will be used will be $\qlt$ for the set $A$, $\qeq$ for the, possibly empty, set $B$, and the set $\qgt$ for $C$. The notation $\qleq$ will be used for the set $\qlt \cup \qeq$ while $\qgeq$ denotes $\qgt \cup \qeq$. To refer to the cut itself, the notation $\cut$ will be used. 



If $\qeq = \{q\}$, then $\qlt$ is the set of rational numbers less than $q$ and $\qgt$ is the set of rational numbers greater than $q$. The notation $\cut[q]$ will represent this cut, noting that $q$ is a rational number. Cuts of this form represent the rational numbers and $q$ is the root of the cut. 

If $\qeq$ is empty, then the cut represents an irrational number. In that case, $\qgt$ and $\qlt$ are complements in the set of rational numbers. 

A common definition of Dedekind cuts is to define them solely in terms of the sets $\qlt$.  A set of rationals $A$ is a \textbf{lower cut} if it satisfies the following three properties: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Not Trivial. $A \neq \emptyset$ and $A \neq \mathbb{Q}$.
    \item Downward Closed. If $p \in A$ and $q < p$, then $q \in A$. 
    \item Open Above. If $p \in A$, then there exists $q \in A$ such that $p < q$.
\end{enumerate}
Using lower cuts as the definition of Dedekind cuts is common and is what is done in \cite{rudin}, page 17. There are also Upper Cuts modeled on $\qgt$. Another version of Dedekind cuts is to use lower and upper cuts, but not the singular set. In the idealized world where everything is decidable, all of these different ways are equivalent. The approach here, similar to the Rational Betweenness Relations, brings to the forefront the difficulty with rationals. 

The following statements are easy to verify: 
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cuts}
Let $x$ be a cut and $A$ a lower cut.  
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*., ref=\thelemma(\alph*)]
    \item\label{lem:cuts-disj} The sets $\qlt$, $\qeq$, $\qgt$ are pairwise disjoint.
    \item If $a \in \qleq$ and $p < a$, then $p \in \qlt$. 
    \item If $b \in \qgeq$ and $b < q$, then $q \in \qgt$. 
    \item If $a \in \qleq$ and $p \leq a$, then $p \in \qleq$. 
    \item If $b \in \qgeq$ and $b \leq q$, then $q \in \qgeq$. 
    \item A cut $y$ is equal to $x$ if and only if $\qlt \subseteq \qlt[y]$ and $\qgt \subseteq \qgt[y]$.
    \item $\qlt$ is a lower cut and $\qgt$ is an upper cut. 
    \item The complement $A^c$ of $A$ is exactly the set of upper bounds of $A$. 
    \item The set $A^c$ is Upward Closed and not finite. 
    
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}\label{br--lowcutdef}
    If a set $A$ is a lower cut, then there is a unique cut $\cut$ such that $A = \qlt$.
\end{proposition}

An overline will be used to indicate the completion of the lower cut to a full cut. In the above, $\overline{A} = \cut$.

\begin{proof}
    Let $A^c$ be the complement of $A$. If there exists $q \in A^c$ such that $q \leq r$ for all $r \in A^c$, then define $B = \{q\}$ and $C = A^c \setminus \{q\}$. If there is no such least number $q$, then define $B = \{\}$ and $C = A^c$. 

    The claim is that $(A, B, C)$ forms a cut. The properties hold as follows:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Nonempty. The Not Trivial property of a lower cut ensures this for both $A$ and its complement. 
        \item Ordered. The complement being upper bounds covers most of this. If $B$ is not empty, then it is the least element of the $C$ and hence less than them. 
        \item Comprehensive. $A \cup B \cup C = A \cup A^c = \mathbb{Q}$ by definition. 
        \item Singular. There is at most one least element of any given set of rationals. 
        \item Open. The Open Above property for $A$ is half of this. The other half follows from having removed the least element of $A^c$ if it exists. If it does not, then $A^c = C$ is already Open Below. If there is a least element $q$, then given any element $r$ in $C$, $\frac{r+q}{2}$ is less than $r$ and is in $C$. 
    \end{enumerate}

    As for uniqueness, any cut such that $A = \qlt$ will have that $A^c = \qgeq$. This set will either have a least element or it will not and that determines whether $\qeq$ is singular or empty, respectively. Either way, $\qeq$ and $\qgt$ are uniquely determined by $\qlt$. 
\end{proof}


\section{From Relation to Cut and Back Again}

This section will establish a canonical bijection between the rational betweenness relations and Dedekind cuts. The term canonical refers to the fact that the natural bijection described below does not require any arbitrary choices. It maps the representative of rationals to each other, e.g., the zero cut is mapped to the zero relation. 

Given the betweenness relation $\xrel$, the associated Dedekind cut is defined by \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\qlt = \{ a | \exists\, b, c \, (a < c \leq b) \wedge (c \xrel b) \} $
    \item $\qgt = \{ b | \exists\, a, c \, (a \leq c < b) \wedge (a \xrel c) \} $
    \item $\qeq = \{ q | q \xrel q \} $
\end{enumerate}
In words, $\qlt$ is the set of non-root lower endpoints of $x$-intervals, $\mathbb{Q}_{>x}$ is the set of non-root upper endpoints of $x$-intervals, and $\qeq$ is the set of endpoints that form singletons in the $x$-between relation. The use of $c$ is present to ensure that if a singleton is present in the relation, then its endpoint does not appear in either of the inequality sets. The set $\qeq$ consists exactly of the roots of the relation; the Singular property asserts that there is at most one root. 

Is this a cut? Yes: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Nonempty. Existence yields an $x$-interval $a \rint b$. Without loss of generality, assume $a \leq b$. Then $a-1< a$, $b+1 > b$. By definition of the cut, $a-1 \in \qlt$ and $b+1 \in \qgt$.
    \item Ordered. Let $a \in \qlt$ and $b \in \qgt$.  Then there exists $c, m$ such that $a < c \leq m$ and $c \xrel m$. There is also a $d, n$ such that $d \leq n < b$  and $d \xrel n$. By Proposition \ref{br--intersect}, $c \xrel m$ and $d \xrel n$ intersect. Let $p$ be an element of that intersection. Then $a < c \leq p \leq n < b$ implying $a < b$.  If $\qeq$ is not empty, then let $q$ be the element of $\qeq$. By Corollary \ref{br--cor--rooted}, $c \xrel m$ and $d \xrel n$ implies that both $a\rint c\rint q\rint m$ and $d\rint q\rint n\rint b$ hold true. Thus, $a < q$ and $q < b$.  
    \item Comprehensive. Let any rational $a$ be given. If $a \xrel a$, then $a \in \qeq$. If not, then Lemma \ref{br--existence} yields the existence of $b$ such that $a \xrel b$. By Inward, there exists a $c$ such that $a\betneq c\rint b$ with $c \xrel b$. Thus, $a \in \qlt$ if $a < c$ while $a  \in \qgt$ if $a > c$. 
    \item Singular. By the Singular property of the relation, there is at most one $q$ such that $q \xrel q$. Thus $\qeq$ is either empty or consists of one element. 
    \item Open. Let $a\in \qlt$ and $b \in \qgt$. The task is to establish that there exists $m \in \qlt, n \in \qgt$ such that $a < m$ and $n < b$. By assumption, there exists an $x$-interval that excludes $a$ from above and an $x$-interval that excludes $b$ from below. By Proposition \ref{br--intersect}, those intervals intersect in an $x$-interval which can be taken to be $c\rint d$ with $c \leq d$. This implies $a\rint c\rint d\rint b$ with $c$ distinct from $a$ and $d$ distinct from $b$. Since $c$ or $d$ could be roots of the relation, it is necessary to look at other elements. Let $m = \frac{a+c}{2}$ and $n = \frac{d+b}{2}$. Then $m \in \qlt$ and $n \in \qgt$ as $c$ and $d$, respectively, still fill the same role as required in the definition. Thus $\qlt$ has no greatest element and $\qgt$ has no least element. 
\end{enumerate}

In the other direction, given the Dedekind $x$-cut, the associated $x$-betweenness relation is defined by, for $a \leq b$, $a \xrel b$ exactly when $a \in \qleq$ and $b \in \qgeq$. 

Is this a betweenness relation? Yes: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Existence. By the Nonempty property, we have the existence of $a \in \qlt$ and $b \in \qgt$ which implies $a \xrel b$. 
    \item Separation. Let $a \xrel b$ with $a \leq b$.  Then $a \in \qleq$ and $b \in \qgeq$. Let $c$ be given such that $a\rint c\rint b$. By the Comprehensive property, there are three possible cases: $c \in \qlt$, $c \in \qgt$, or $c \in \qeq$. If $c \in \qlt$, then  $c \xrel b$. It is also the case that \sout{$a \xrel c$} as both $a$ and $c$ are in $\qlt$ implying neither of them are in $\qgeq$. Similarly, if $c \in \qgt$, then \sout{$c \xrel b$} and $a \xrel c$. If $c \in \qeq$, then $c \in \qleq$ and $c \in \qgeq$ which implies $c \xrel c$. 
    \item Consistency. Let $c\rint a \rint b\rint d$ with $ a \xrel b$. The claim is that $c \xrel d$. Without loss of generality, assume $a \leq b$ which implies $a \in \qleq$ and $b \in \qgeq$.  The betweenness leads to $c \leq a$ and $ b \leq d$. By Lemma \ref{br--cut-unbounded},  $c \in \qleq$ and $ d \in \qgeq$. Thus, $c \xrel d$.
    \item Singular. The three pieces of the cut are disjoint. The only way to get an $x$-singleton $q\rint q$ is if $q \in \qeq$. That set has at most one element. 
    \item Closed. Assume that $c$ is such that, for any $a$, $b$, if $a \xrel b$, then $a\rint c\rint b$. Since the three cut sets disjointly span the rationals by the Comprehensive property, $c$ is in exactly one of those sets. If $c \in \qlt$, then, by the Open property, there exists $a \in \qlt$ such that $c < a$. Take any $b \in \qgt$ which can be done by the Nonempty property. Then $a \xrel b$ by definition while \sout{$a\rint c\rint b$} contradicting the assumption about $c$. Similarly, if $c \in \qgt$, then there would exist $b \in \qgt$ with $b < c$ and any $a \in \qlt$ would lead to \sout{$a\rint c\rint b$} with $a \xrel b$. With neither of these options available, it must be the case that $c \in \qeq$ implying $c \xrel c$.    
\end{enumerate}

The final task is to establish that these two are bijections and, in particular, they are the inverses of one another. 

To show the mapping from relations to cuts is one-to-one, let $\xrel$ and $\yrel$ represent two distinct rational betweenness relations. By Proposition \ref{br--different}, there exists $x$-interval $a \rint b$ and $y$-interval $c\rint d$ such that they are disjoint. Without loss of generality, assume that $a \leq b < c \leq d$. Then $c \in \qleq[y]$, $d \in \qgeq[y]$ while $c, d \in \qgt[x]$. Thus, different relations are mapped to different cuts. 

To show the mapping from the relations to the cuts is onto, the path will be to show that the cut-to-relation mapping is undone by the relation-to-cut mapping. Let a cut $\cut$ be given. Let $\yrel$ represent the relation that the cut is mapped to. Let $\cut[z]$ represent the cut that $\yrel$ is mapped to. The goal is to show that $\cut = \cut[z]$.  

Let $a \in \qlt[z]$ and $b \in \qgt[z]$. The main task is to show $a \in \qlt$ and $b \in \qgt$. By the definition of the relation-to-cut mapping, there exists $m_a < n_a, m_b < n_b$ such that $m_a \xrel[y] n_a$, $a < m_a$, and $m_b \xrel[y] n_b$ with $n_b < b$. The two $x$-intervals intersect in an $x$-interval $c\rint d$ by  Proposition \ref{br--intersect}. Without loss of generality, assume $c \leq d$. By transitivity, it is then the case that $a < c \leq d < b$ and $c \yrel d$. By the definition of the cut-to-relation mapping, $c \in \qleq$ and $d \in \qgeq$. Because $a < c$ and $ d < b$, $a \in \qlt$ and $b \in \qgt$ by Lemma \ref{br--cut-unbounded}. Thus, $\qlt[z] \subseteq \qlt$ and $\qgt[z] \subseteq \qgt$. By Lemma \ref{br--cut-equal}, $\cut[z] = \cut$

% The only set left to explore is $\qeq[z]$. This is two parts. If $\qeq[z]$ is empty, then that implies that $c\rint c$ is not a $y$-interval for all rationals $c$. Since singletons $c\rint c$ are a $y$-interval exactly when $c$ is in both $\qleq$ and $\qgeq$, that intersection is empty. Since the elements of $\qeq$ are common to both unions, this implies $\qeq$ is empty. The other part is if $c \in \qeq[z]$. This means $c \yrel c$. But that means that $c$ was an element of the intersection of $\qleq$ and $\qgeq$. Since $\qlt$, $\qeq$, and $\qgt$ are disjoint, this implies $c \in \qeq$ as was needed to be shown. This also shows that, for any given rational $q$, the $q$-cut is mapped to the $q$-relation. 

Thus, given a cut, it maps to a relation that maps to the given cut. The two mappings are inverses to each other.

The above proves 
\begin{theorem}
    The set of rational Dedekind cuts is in canonical bijective correspondence with the set of rational betweenness relations. The representative of the rational $q$ in the relations does correspond to the cut associated with $q$.
\end{theorem}

From this point on, using $x$ in the relation notation $\xrel$ and the cut notation $\cut$, or in the sets of the cut $(\qlt, \qeq, \qgt)$, implies that they are related by this canonical bijection. 

\section{Ordering the Relations}

Dedekind cuts are a complete ordered field as shown in, for example, \cite{rudin}. Rudin uses the lower cuts and this readily translates into this paper's version of Dedekind cuts due to Proposition \ref{br--lowcutdef}. To establish that  the betweenness relations are a complete ordered field, the path will be to show that the natural operations on the betweenness relations is mapped to those operations on their associated cuts. This section will be on the order relations.  

Given two intervals $a \rint b$ and $c\rint d$, the notion that $a \rint b < c\rint d$ is that given any $p$ and $q$ such that $a\rint p\rint b$ and $c\rint q\rint d$, it can be concluded that $p < q$. This includes the endpoints and can be determined by comparing endpoints. Indeed, if $a\leq b$ and $c \leq d$, then $a \rint b < c\rint d$ exactly when $b < c$. Transitivity of interval inequality can readily be shown to hold true. 

From the rational betweenness relation perspective, $x < y$ occurs, by definition, exactly when there exists an $x$-interval $a \rint b$ and a $y$-interval $c\rint d$ such that $a \rint b < c\rint d$. By Proposition \ref{br--different}, given two distinct relations $\xrel$ and $\yrel$, there exists disjoint intervals $a \rint b$ and $c\rint d$ such that $a \xrel b$ and $c \yrel d$. Disjointness means that one of the intervals is wholly greater than the other. By Proposition \ref{br--intersect}, that order relation cannot be reversed. For example, if $a \xrel b$, $c \xrel[y] d$, $u \yrel v$, and $a \rint b < c\rint d$, then $u:v \nless a \rint b$ as $u\rint v \nless c\rint d$ as $u\rint v$ and $c\rint d$ intersect.  To emphasize it as a relation, the inequality may be denoted as $\xrel  \:<\: \yrel$. 

If both relations happen to be  rooted with roots $p$ and $q$, respectively to the relations $\xrel$ and $\yrel$, then $x<y$ if and only if $p < q$ if and only if $p\rint p < q\rint q$. 

For Dedekind cuts, $x < y$ occurs, by definition, exactly when $\qlt \subsetneq \qlt[y]$. This would imply that $\qgt[y] \subset \qgt$. For cuts representing rationals, the solitary elements of $\qeq$ and $\qeq[y]$ will have that ordering though there is no set relation that expresses it. To emphasize it as a cut, the inequality may be denoted as $\cut < \cut[y]$.

The ordering definition of Dedekind cuts is well-established to obey all the desired properties. The task here is to show that the relation ordering and the Dedekind ordering correspond under the bijection which implies the relation ordering also has the same desired properties. 

Assume that $\xrel  \:<\: \yrel$ which implies there exists an $x$-interval $a \rint b$, $a \leq b$,  and a $y$-interval $c\rint d$, $c \leq d$, such that $a \rint b < c\rint d$. Then $a \in \qleq$, $b \in \qgeq$, $c \in \qleq[y]$, $d \in \qgeq[y]$. Since $ b< c$, Lemma \ref{br--cut-unbounded} implies $b \in \qlt[y]$. Since all elements of $\qlt$ are less than $b$, $\qlt \subset \qlt[y]$. Because $b\notin \qlt$ while $b \in \qlt[y]$, it is that case that $\qlt \neq \qlt[y]$. Thus,  $\cut < \cut[y]$. 

For the other direction, assume $\cut < \cut[y]$. By the meaning of the inequality, there exists $b \in \qlt[y]$ which is not in $\qlt$; this implies $b \in \qgeq$. Let $a \in \qlt$ and $d \in \qgt[y]$.  Because there is no greatest element of $\qlt[y]$, let $c\in \qlt[y]$ such that $b < c$. By the definition of the bijection,  $a \xrel b$ and $c \yrel d$. Because $a < b < c < d$, it is the case that $a \rint b < c\rint d$ so that $\xrel  \:<\: \yrel$, as was to be shown. 

\begin{theorem}
For relations $\xrel$ and $\yrel$, $\xrel  \:<\: \yrel$ if and only if $\cut < \cut[y]$.
\end{theorem}


With an ordering, the least upper bound of a set can be queried as to whether it exists or not. Lower cuts give an elegant way to define the least upper bound. Given a non-empty set $E$ of cuts that is bounded above by a cut $z$, then the least upper bound $\alpha$ of $E$ is defined by its lower cut $\qlt[\alpha] = \bigcup_{x \in E} \qlt$. It is straightforward to establish that this is a lower cut, that the cut $\alpha$ is an upper bound, and it is the least upper bound. This is done in detail in the previously cited Rudin \cite{rudin} and establishes that Dedekind cuts are complete. 

 For the relations, the bijection maps a bounded set of relations $\tilde{E}$ to a bounded set of cuts $E$. The least upper bound $\cut[\alpha]$ of $E$ maps to a relation $\xrel[\alpha]$. Because the bijection respects the ordering, the image will be the least upper bound relation of $\tilde{E}$. 

\begin{corollary}
    The set of rational betweenness relations with the interval ordering satisfies the least upper bound property. 
\end{corollary}

By definition of the bijection, $a \xrel[\alpha] b$, $a \leq b$, exactly when $a \in \qleq [\alpha]$ and $b \in \qgeq[\alpha]$. In words, $b$ is a rational upper bound of $E$ while $a$ is a rational lower bound of the set of upper bounds of $E$. This is what one would expect of the least upper bound of the relations. 

A similar bijection dance can be used to establish that the rationals are dense in the relations. But it is rather simple to see it directly as well. For rational betweenness relations, given $\xrel < \yrel$, the task is to show that there is a rational between those two. The inequality implies there exists $a,b,c,d$ such that $a \xrel b$, $c \xrel[y] d$, and $a \rint b \betneq c\rint d$ with $b < c$. Let $m = \frac{b+c}{2}$ be the average of $b$ and $c$. This is a rational number and the relation $\xrel[m]$ satisfies $\xrel  \:<\: \xrel[m]  \:<\: \xrel[y]$ because $a \rint b < m\rint m < c\rint d$ due to $b < m < c$. Thus, the rooted relations are dense in the ordered set of betweenness relations. 

\begin{corollary}
    The set of rooted rational betweenness relations is dense in the set of rational betweenness relations.
\end{corollary}
 

\section{The Arithmetic of Relations}

Having established the canonical bijection between cuts and relations and that the ordered properties work, the final step to do is the arithmetic. As fully proving arithmetic can be a little bit complicated, this paper will take the shortcut of using Dedekind cut arithmetic.

\begin{theorem}
The canonical bijection between cuts and rational betweenness relations is an isomorphism which establishes that the rational betweenness relations form a complete, ordered field with the rational numbers as a dense subset. 
\end{theorem}


The approach will be that the field structure on rational betweenness relations is obtained by transporting the field structure on cuts across the canonical bijection.

\begingroup

\providecommand{\tuple}[2][n]{{}^{#1} #2}


 \noindent\textbf{Convention.} For this proof, if $E_i$ is an indexed expression, then $\tuple{E_i}$ denotes the ordered n-tuple whose $i$-th entry is $E_i$. For example, $\tuple{\qlt[x_i]}$ is a tuple of $n$ lower cuts.

\begin{proof}
    Let $O\cut[<]$ be an arithmetic operator on the space of lower cuts. By \cite{rudin}, pages 17-21, the usual arithmetic operators satisfy the properties of an ordered field. For $n$-ary operators, define $O\cut[](
    \tuple{\cut[x_i]} ) = \overline{O\cut[<]( \tuple{\qlt[x_i]} )}$. In words, take the lower cut of the given cuts, operate on them, then use the completion to cuts via Proposition \ref{br--lowcutdef}. Since the completion to cuts is canonical, equality is preserved under this correspondence.

    Define operators on relations via operators on cuts by using $\xrel[O](\tuple{\!\xrel[x_i]}) = \xrel[z]$ where 
    $\cut[z] = O\cut[](\tuple{\cut[x_i]})$.  As the field properties hold on the arithmetic of the cuts, the properties transfer to the operators on the relations. For example, the commutativity of addition schematically comes from  
    \[
    \begin{aligned}
     \xrel + \xrel[y] &\longrightarrow \cut + \cut[y] \\
     &\longrightarrow \qlt + \qlt[y] \\ 
     &= \qlt[y] + \qlt \\
     &\longrightarrow \cut[y] + \cut \\
     &\longrightarrow \xrel[y] + \xrel   
    \end{aligned}
    \]
     where the equality is established by \cite{rudin}. To do this with the operator notation, let $O(x,y) = x + y$ and $P(x,y)= y + x$. Then the goal is to show $\xrel[O](\xrel, \xrel[y]) = \xrel[P](\xrel, \xrel[y])$ which will follow from $O\cut[<](\qlt[x],\qlt[y]) = P\cut[<] (\qlt[x], \qlt[y])$ as argued below. 
        
    In general, let $O$ and $P$ be two operators such that $O\cut[<](\tuple{\qlt[x_i]}) = P\cut[<](\tuple{\qlt[x_i]})$. Then the goal is to show $\xrel[O](\tuple{\!\xrel[x_i]}) = \xrel[P](\tuple{\!\xrel[x_i]})$ which will hold true, by definition, if and only if $O\cut[](\tuple{\cut[x_i]}) =P\cut[](\tuple{\cut[x_i]}) $, which, in turn, holds if and only if $\overline{O\cut[<](\tuple{\qlt[x_i]})} = \overline{P\cut[<](\tuple{\qlt[x_i]})}$. The last equality follows from  the starting assumption that  $O\cut[<](\tuple{\qlt[x_i]}) = P\cut[<](\tuple{\qlt[x_i]})$.

    In what follows, $\cdot$ will stand in generically for $+$ and $*$. The notation $x^{-1}$ for $\cdot$ will stand in for $-x$ and $\frac{1}{x}$ for $+$ and $*$, respectively. The number $1$ will represent the identity for the generic operator.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Commutativity. $O(x,y) = x \cdot y$ and $P(x,y)= y \cdot x$
        \item Associativity. $O(x,y,z)= (x\cdot y) \cdot z$ and $P(x,y,z)=x \cdot (y \cdot z)$
        \item Distributive.  $O(x,y,z)= x*(y+x)$ and $P(x,y,z)= (x*y) + (x*z)$
        \item Identity. $O(x) = 1 \cdot x$ and $P(x)= x$
        \item Inverse. $O(x) = x \cdot x^{-1}$ and $P(x)= 1$.
    \end{itemize}
    
    Apply the equality argument above to all of them noting that the lower cut equality for each of them has been established in Rudin. 

    The rest of the theorem was established earlier. 
\end{proof}
\endgroup

While the above suffices, some exploration of what it means for intervals is useful. The arithmetic of the relations  can be described as doing the arithmetic on the endpoints of intervals. This creates a core of intervals which can then be expanded to the full relation. 

To make the correspondence with Dedekind cuts easier, the intervals in the core of the arithmetic will be taken to be Spaced intervals. An $x$-\textbf{Spaced interval} is an $x$-related interval $a \rint b$ such that neither $a$ nor $b$ are roots of the relation. If the labeling is such that $a \leq b$, then it can be concluded that $a < b$, $a \in \qlt$ and $b \in \qgt$.

In what follows, the explicit correspondence of the core intervals to the lower cuts will be explored.  At times, it is necessary to focus on $x > 0$ at which point the notation $\qlt^+$ is useful and it denotes the positive elements of the set of $\qlt$. It is also important to note that having a defined $\qlt^+$ does then yield a unique lower cut, corresponding to $\qlt$, by taking the union with $\qleq[0]$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Addition. $u \xrel[x+y] v$ if it is an interval of the form $(a+c)\rint (b+d)$ where $a \rint b$ is an $x$-Spaced interval, $c\rint d$ is a $y$-Spaced interval, $a < b$, and $c < d$. The definition of cut addition is based on $\qlt[x+y] = \{a + c | a \in \qlt, c \in \qlt[y]\} $. This is exactly the lower endpoints of the core addition intervals. 
    \item Negation. $u \xrel[-x] v$ if it is an interval of the form $-b\rint -a$ where $a \rint b$ is an $x$-Spaced interval. The definition of cut negation is based on $\qlt[-x] = \{-b | b \in \qgt\}$. This is exactly the lower endpoints of the core negation intervals. 
    \item Multiplication. $u \xrel[xy] v$  if it is an interval of the form $m\rint M$ where $m = \min(ac, bc, ad, bd)$, $M = \max(ac, bc, ad, bd)$, $a \rint b$ is an $x$-Spaced interval, and $c\rint d$ is a $y$-Spaced interval. For $0 \leq a < b$ and $0 \leq c < d$, this translates to $ac\rint bd$. To correspond to Dedekind cuts, only the positive version is done directly even though the relation version has no need for such a restriction. The other versions are handled by imposing the usual negation and zero multiplication rules. Restricting to the case of $x , y > 0$, the lower cut of $xy$ is based on $\qlt[xy]^+ = \{ac | a \in \qlt^+, c \in \qlt[y]^+ \}$. This is exactly the lower endpoints of the core multiplication intervals of positive numbers. 
    \item Reciprocation. $u \xrel[\frac{1}{x}] v$ if it is an interval of the form $\frac{1}{b}\rint \frac{1}{a}$ where $a \rint b$ is an $x$-Spaced interval which does not contain 0. This is restricted to non-zero numbers. Since one can double negate, with reciprocating in between, it is sufficient to discuss positive cuts. For $x> 0$, the set $\qlt[1/x]^+ = \{\frac{1}{b} | b \in \qgt\}$ which is exactly the set of lower endpoints of the core reciprocation intervals. 
\end{enumerate}


In this approach, the operation of powering to a natural number is taken to be repeated multiplication as that requires no further work. But this can be shown to be equivalent to directly defining the power operation with the core intervals being $a^n \xrel[x^n] b^n$ where $a \rint b$ is an $x$-Spaced interval that does not contain 0. Since the notation is betweenness notation, no ordering of $a^n \rint b^n$ is implied. For even powers, the exclusion of 0 keeps the powering monotone ensuring the endpoints remain endpoints after applying the power. For $x = 0$, $0^n$ is defined as $0$. 


\section{Conclusion}

Rational Betweenness Relations have been established to be a model of real numbers using Dedekind Cuts. The path in this paper is not the only way to establish this. In \cite{taylor24oraclerbr}, the approach is to define another kind of object, called an oracle, which answers Yes or No when given an interval; the intent is that Yes means the real number it represents is in the given interval. It has similar properties to the Rational Betweenness Relations, but allows for a fuzzier, more indeterminate nature. It is closer to numerical work and helps with the actual computational difficulties of dealing with real numbers and the inability to say when two real numbers are definitively equal. 

Using intervals to define real numbers is not new.  A nested sequence of intervals being a real number was proposed by Bachmann not too long after the use of Cauchy sequences and Dedekind cuts. The constructivists, as conveyed by Mark Bridger \cite{bridger}, generalize that approach and use fine families of intervals where are families of intersecting, arbitrarily small intervals to represent real numbers. Both of those approaches lack uniqueness of representatives. The oracles mentioned above are equivalent to the fine families.

There are many definitions of real numbers as surveyed in the excellent overview by Ittay Weiss \cite{ittay-2015}. They have various strengths and weaknesses such as multiple representatives for a given real number or difficulty in handling approximations. Many, such as Dedekind cuts, rely on some particulars of rational numbers that prevent generalization to other spaces. Relations can be generalized to other spaces using containers; the Separation Property is changed to say that given two points in a container, then there exists a subcontainer excluding at least one of the points. Other real number definitions, such as Cauchy sequences, have almost a misleading quality about them, such as having the initial segment of it being completely arbitrary while the infinite tail is not something that one can generally write down. An attempt to tighten that down using tighter bounds on the epsilon condition then leads more to an interval outlook and also tends to complicate arithmetic. 

One particular strength of the relations and related interval definitions, is that they carry definiteness with them naturally. Think about computing a real number for some other computation. The relation approach naturally leads to using intervals. If one has a specific length requirement, the tools and structures required to get to that level come with the relation definition itself. For cuts, it has to be, admittedly rather easily, built up. The cut structure itself seems less relevant. This may happen with definitions, but it is preferable to have definitions that align with the use of the object. 

In particular, imagine trying to get close to $\pi$ and let's say it is known that $\pi$ is between 3 and 4. For the relations, this is asserting that $3 \xrel[\pi] 4$. It is just a restatement of that fact. For the cuts, it would be the assertion that $3 \in \qlt[\pi]$ and $4 \in \qgt[\pi]$. It feels as if the message of the cuts is that we are filling up the sets $\qlt[\pi]$ and $\qgt[\pi]$. It has the feeling of looking away from the real number rather than the relations viewpoint which is to enclose the real number. 

Rational betweenness relations are a new way of perceiving the real numbers. They give a clear and accessible definition while also being able to help with computing out the number. They encourage precise thinking about the imprecision associated with a real number. The concept also has the promise of generalizations to many other spaces. They present a way for how finite beings can meaningfully claim knowledge of an inherently infinite real number.

Having established that the rational betweenness relations form a maximal fonsi, the next task is to establish that every fonsi uniquely generates a maximal fonsi that is a rational betweenness relation. 

\begin{lemma}
    A fonsi is contained in at most one maximal fonsi. 
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{G}$ be two maximal fonsis such that $\mathcal{H}$ is a fonsi contained in each of them. Let $a \rint b$ be any interval in $\mathcal{F}$ which implies it intersects every interval in $\mathcal{H}$. Let $c \rint d$ be any interval in $\mathcal{G}$ which implies it intersects every interval in $\mathcal{H}$. If $c \rint d$ is disjoint from $a \rint b$, then without loss of generality, $a \rint b \betneq c \rint d$. Let $\varepsilon = |b-c|/2$. Since $\mathcal{H}$ is a fonsi, there exists an interval $e \rint f$ whose length is less than $\varepsilon$ and is contained in $\mathcal{H}$. It must therefore intersect both $a \rint b$ and $c \rint d$, but the length prevents that. Thus, $a \rint b$ must intersect $c \rint d$. Since both were arbitrary intervals in there fonsis, every interval of $\mathcal{F}$ must intersect every interval of $\mathcal{G}$. Since these are maximal fonsis, they must contain the same intervals and are therefore the same fonsi. 
\end{proof}



\begin{proposition}
    Every fonsi generates a unique maximal fonsi that is a rational betweenness relation.
\end{proposition}

Conceptually, the relation is the set of all intervals that contain an element of the fonsi in addition to the closure of including any singleton whose single number is contained in all elements of the fonsi. The approach below is a little bit more refined than that, but this is the goal of the construction. 

The closed $\delta$-halo of $a \rint b$ is defined to be the interval $c \rint a \rint b \rint d$ such that $|c-a|=|b-d|=\delta$; it is denoted by $\clhalo{a \rint b}$.

\begin{proof}
    The above lemma establishes uniqueness and it was already established that every rational betweenness relation is a a maximal fonsi. 

    Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a given fonsi. Define $\xrel$ by $a \xrel b$ if for each $\delta > 0$, the closed $\delta$ halo of $a \rint b$ intersects an element of the fonsi whose size is less than $\delta$. Since every element of the fonsi intersects every other element of the fonsi and there are arbitrarily small elements of the fonsi, every element of the fonsi is an $x$ interval. The properties of the relation are established as follows: 

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Existence. By definition, fonsis are not empty and every element of the fonsi is in the relation. 

        
        \item Separation. Let $a \xrel b$ and $a \rint c \rint b$ be given. If $c \xrel c$, then Separation is satisfied. If not, then there there exists $\delta > 0$ and a fonsi element $m \rint n$ such that $\clhalo{c \rint c}$ does not intersect $m \rint n$. Without loss of generality, assume $m \rint n \rint c$ and $\{a, m, n\} \rint c$. Let $\varepsilon$ be the distance from $n$ to the closest endpoint of $\clhalo{c \rint c}$. The lemma implies that any fonsi element whose size is less than $\varepsilon$ are all on the same side of $\clhalo{c \rint c}$ and does not intersect $\clhalo{c \rint c}$. 
        Thus, they are also disjoint from all $\clhalo{c \rint b}$ which implies \sout{$c \xrel b$}. 
        
        For every $\delta' > 0$, the fact that $a \xrel b$ implies the existence of an element of the fonsi that intersects $\clhalo[\delta']{a \rint b}$. If $\delta' < \varepsilon$, then the intersection must be in $\clhalo[\delta']{a \rint b} \setminus \clhalo[\delta']{c \rint b}$ which is a subset of $\clhalo[\delta']{a \rint c}$. Thus, the same small fonsi elements provide witness for $a \xrel c$. 
        
        \item Consistency. If $a \xrel b$ and $c \rint a \rint b \rint d$, then, as $\clhalo{c \rint d}$ contains $\clhalo{a \rint b}$ for all $\delta$, it has the same qualifying intersections to be in the relation.
        \item Singular. Let $a \xrel a$ and $b \neq a$ be given. Let $\delta=|a-b|/4$. Let $c \rint d$ be an element of the fonsi whose length is than $\delta$ and intersects $\clhalo{a \rint a}$; this exists by definition of $a \xrel a$. Then $c \rint d$ cannot intersect any interval whose length is less than $\delta$ and intersects $\clhalo{b \rint b}$. This implies no element of the fonsi whose length is less than $\delta$ can intersect $\clhalo{b \rint b}$ and thus \sout{$b \xrel b$}.
        \item Inward. If \sout{$a \xrel a$}, then there exists $\delta' >0$ such that any element of the fonsi whose size is less than $\delta'$ will not intersect $\clhalo[\delta']{a \rint a}$. By the lemma, all elements of the fonsi that are small enough not to intersect that interval will be on the same side of $a$. Define $c$ to be the endpoint of $\clhalo{a \rint a}$ on that side of $a$. 
        
        Let $a \xrel b$ which implies for any $\delta > 0$, there exists a fonsi element whose size is less than $\delta$ and intersects $\clhalo{a \rint b}$.  But for $\delta < \delta'$, as no fonsi whose size is less than $\delta'$ will intersect $\clhalo[\delta']{a \rint a}$, it must be the case that $a \betneq c \rint b$ and that those elements are intersecting $\clhalo{c \rint b}$. Thus, $c \xrel b$. 
    \end{enumerate}

    
\end{proof}
